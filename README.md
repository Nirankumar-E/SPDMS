SMART PUBLIC DISTRIBUTION MANAGEMENT SYSTEM

The Public Distribution System is really important, and it was introduced for helping people who are underprivileged and for economically weaker sections, but it has these ongoing problems like big crowds at the distribution centres (ration shops), not enough clear information on the goods allocation and availability, services that take too long, and everything still done mostly manually. That stuff makes things harder for the people waiting in long lines for plenty of hours and slows down how the whole system works overall. Some solutions out there, like the ones in papers, include digitising the public ration distribution system using an Android application integrated with RFID technology to ensure secure, transparent, and efficient delivery of ration items. It minimises manual intervention and fraud by enabling automated user authentication and real-time monitoring through a microcontroller-based system. Try to fix parts of it with stuff like RFID tags or smart cards, or even linking to Aadhaar for ID checks and IoT for handling inventory. They handle one thing at a time, authentication or stock management, but nothing ties it all together. So, you still get delays and things running inefficiently because there’s no way to schedule when people come or keep track of everything from start to finish or let users have more control. So, in this paper, I’m suggesting something called the Smart Public Distribution Management System, or SPDMS, which is like a digital way to run things better, focusing on making sure everything is open and accountable and getting operations to run smoother. It uses a safe setup that handles checking who the beneficiaries are by using the concept of Simple Queue System and Simple Notification System, scheduling times so not everyone shows up at once, doing transactions digitally, and verifying with QR codes and biometrics. By changing how distribution happens into steps that are checked ahead and planned out, it cuts down on people having to do things manually and avoids those jams in the process. At the shop, the QR code and biometrics check make validation quick, and they keep track of all the transactions too, which seems useful. Other systems before this one mostly stuck to automating with hardware or just basic ID checks, but this one pushes for more openness in the process, limits who can access what, and schedules around what users need, all to make public services work better. In tests they did with simulated setups, it cut waiting times for people by around 40 to 50 percent, errors in manual checks down by almost 60 percent, and crowd levels at the shops by about 45 percent, compared to the old ways or half-automated ones. The model here could work as a starting point to go digital in welfare programmes, especially where resources are limited, though I’m not totally sure how easily that scales everywhere.
